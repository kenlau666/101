# AI Consulting — Phase 2 Course (From Engagement to Firm)

> From Consultant to Consultancy.
> Four lessons. Teaching mode is gentle and explains every term. Drills are harsh.
> Assumes AI Consulting 101 in full: problem extraction, the kill decision, the task grid, the autonomy dial, paid discovery, the ROI equation, eval-gated SOWs, the red-lines workshop, and the retainer. This course never re-explains them. It asks a different question: you can run one engagement well — how do you run twelve, concurrently, without being in every room, while the whole thing compounds instead of consuming you?

---

## Prerequisites

You have 101 cold, and ideally one or two real engagements behind you (even small ones). Every mechanism in this course assumes the 101 artifacts exist — the discovery document, the scoring grid, the SOW clauses, the retainer scorecard. Phase 2 does not add new client-facing moves. It industrializes the ones you have.

You do not need to have hired anyone or registered a company. You do need to be honest about arithmetic, because this course is mostly arithmetic about yourself.

## Before You Start: What Changes in Phase 2

A Phase 1 practice is, at heart, **one person selling one engagement at a time**. Every unit of revenue costs a unit of your attention. That model has a hard ceiling — your calendar — and a hidden instability: when you are delivering, you are not selling, so revenue arrives in waves separated by droughts you created yourself.

Phase 2 is the escape from that trap, and it rests on one idea borrowed straight from your engineering training: **a firm is a production system, and you are currently its only component — a single point of failure with no redundancy, no caching, and no horizontal scaling.** The remedies are the same as in systems: templatize (cache the repeated work), delegate (add workers), instrument (evals on your own delivery, not just the client's), and decouple (revenue that arrives without your hours attached).

Four lessons:

1. **Positioning and pipeline** — why "AI consultant" is a commodity label in free fall, and how to become the only sane choice for a specific someone.
2. **Multi-engagement operations** — the engagement kit, leverage, and quality control when you are no longer in the room.
3. **The portfolio and the P&L** — the arithmetic of a services firm: utilization, the feast-famine cycle, cash, and the services-to-product transition.
4. **Trust at scale and exit paths** — the engagement that fails anyway, IP boundaries, channels, and the three endgames your earlier choices silently lock in.

```
  PHASE 1 (101)                      PHASE 2 (102)
  +--------------------------+      +----------------------------------+
  | you -> engagement        |      | positioning -> pipeline          |
  |                          |      |      |                           |
  | sell, deliver, retain    | ---> |      v                           |
  | (serially, personally)   |      | kit + people -> N engagements    |
  |                          |      |      |                           |
  | revenue = your hours     |      |      v                           |
  +--------------------------+      | portfolio P&L -> endgame         |
                                    +----------------------------------+
```

## Glossary (firm-economics terms used throughout)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Utilization** | % of your available hours that are billable. The master variable of services economics. |
| **Realization** | % of billable hours actually invoiced and collected (vs written off, discounted, disputed). |
| **Blended rate** | Average revenue per delivered hour across everyone who delivers. |
| **Leverage** | Ratio of others' delivery hours to yours. Zero leverage = you are the firm. |
| **Engagement kit** | Your templated set of 101 artifacts: reusable, versioned, improving after every project. |
| **Productized service** | A fixed-scope, fixed-price, named offering ("the AI Readiness Audit") sold like a product. |
| **SKU** | A distinct sellable unit. A productized service is a SKU. "Custom consulting" is not. |
| **Pipeline** | Prospects at each stage: lead -> discovery -> proposal -> engagement. |
| **CAC** | Customer acquisition cost — everything it costs to win one client. |
| **WIP** | Work in progress — delivered value not yet invoiced. Where services cash goes to die. |
| **Concentration risk** | Too much revenue from one client. They own you; they eventually know it. |
| **Key-person discount** | The valuation haircut a firm takes because it dies without its founder. |
| **Feast-famine cycle** | Sell, then deliver (and stop selling), then starve, then panic-sell. The default rhythm of solo consulting. |

# Lesson 1 — Positioning: Escaping the Commodity Label

## Why This Lesson Exists

In early 2023, "prompt engineer" was the most hyped job title on Earth. Bloomberg profiled postings offering up to US$335,000; LinkedIn filled with overnight prompt-engineering agencies; every freelancing platform sprouted thousands of AI consultants whose entire offer was proximity to a model anyone could rent for twenty dollars a month. By 2024–25 the correction was public: surveys of hiring plans put "prompt engineer" near the bottom of roles companies intended to fill, the trade press ran obituaries for the title, and the skill had been absorbed into everyone's job description — while the models themselves got better at prompting than the prompters. The generalist agencies built on that label did not consolidate. They evaporated.

The mechanism is worth naming precisely, because it is aimed at you. When your positioning is "I know AI," your differentiation erodes at the speed of model improvement and tutorial publication — the two fastest-moving forces in the economy. You are competing with every developer, every laid-off marketer with a certificate, and increasingly with the model itself. **A label that anyone can claim and the technology itself keeps absorbing is not a position; it is a queue.** This lesson is about standing somewhere the queue isn't.

## 1.1 The Commodity Trap, Formally

A prospect can only pay a premium for a difference they can perceive *before* buying. Consulting is a credence good — quality is hard to judge even after delivery, let alone before — so prospects fall back on proxies: specificity, evidence, and referrals. Test your own positioning against the substitution exercise:

```
  "We are an AI consultancy helping businesses         <- swap in any competitor's
   leverage AI to improve efficiency."                    name; sentence still true?
                                                          then it says NOTHING.

  "We build eval-gated WhatsApp order-handling          <- swap test fails: this is
   systems for Hong Kong F&B and event operators,         checkable, specific, and
   priced against the staff-hours they replace."          excludes 99% of competitors
```

The narrower statement terrifies beginners because it seems to shrink the market. It does the opposite, through three mechanisms: **referability** (nobody refers "an AI guy"; everyone refers "THE guy for X" — word of mouth requires a compressible label), **pattern accumulation** (ten engagements in one vertical make the eleventh discovery twice as fast and twice as accurate — your task grids start pre-filled, which is margin), and **price insulation** (a commodity is compared on rate; a specialist is compared on payback, and you learned in 101 which argument you win).

## 1.2 Choosing the Position

Three viable axes; pick primarily one:

1. **Vertical** — an industry: F&B, events, logistics, clinics. You speak their nouns; discovery accelerates; referrals travel inside the industry's own network.
2. **Capability** — a technical wedge: "evals and AI assurance," "WhatsApp/voice agent delivery," "RAG over regulated documents." Travels across industries; competes more directly with tooling.
3. **Problem** — a recurring pain: "founder-bottleneck removal for <50-person firms," "customer-response time." Resonates hardest in the first meeting; hardest to make findable.

Selection is empirical, not aspirational — audit your last N engagements and leads: where did they cluster, where was delivery smoothest, where did the retainer stick, which client would you clone? Then write the position as a falsifiable sentence: **who exactly + what outcome + how it's proven + why you.** The "how it's proven" slot is where your 101 machinery becomes marketing: *"acceptance defined by evaluation scores agreed before we build"* is a sentence almost no competitor can say, because almost no competitor can do it. Your engineering discipline is not backstage — it is the storefront.

One positioning rule that prevents most beginner damage: **narrow the marketing, not the revenue.** You may still take the odd out-of-niche engagement that walks in the door (cash is cash); you never *advertise* the breadth. The website says the narrow sentence. The bank account can be more forgiving.

## 1.3 Productized Services: The SKU Ladder

"Custom consulting" is infinitely flexible and therefore unsellable at scale — every deal needs a bespoke conversation, a bespoke proposal, a bespoke price defense. The fix is to package the 101 artifacts you already run into **named, fixed-scope, fixed-price SKUs** arranged as an ascending ladder of commitment:

```
  SKU LADDER (each rung de-risks the next; prices illustrative)

  rung 0  CONTENT / TEACHING          free      proof of judgment (1.4)
  rung 1  AI READINESS AUDIT          ~HK$15-30k  2-3 days: task grid over their
                                                  ops + build-vs-buy + kill list
  rung 2  PAID DISCOVERY (101 L2)     ~HK$40-80k  the full document, kill
                                                  criteria, pilot quote inside
  rung 3  PILOT (101 L3)              fixed      timeboxed, eval-gated
  rung 4  PRODUCTION BUILD            fixed/T&M  scoped by rung 2-3 outputs
  rung 5  RETAINER (101 L4)           monthly    the business model
```

Why this shape works commercially: each rung is **cheap enough to buy without a committee, valuable standalone, and generative of the next rung's scope** — the audit's kill list is literally the discovery pitch; discovery contains the pilot quote; the pilot manufactures the eval dataset the build is gated on. The prospect never faces a six-figure leap of faith; you never write a speculative proposal again — proposals become "here is rung N+1, priced, as defined by the rung-N document you already own." Operationally, fixed SKUs are also what make delegation possible in Lesson 2: you cannot hand off "custom consulting"; you can absolutely hand off a templated rung-1 audit.

Two design rules. First, every SKU must end in an artifact the client keeps regardless of whether they continue — 101's paid-discovery logic, generalized. Second, the ladder must be **descendible**: a prospect who balks at discovery gets offered the audit, not a discount. Same rate, smaller scope — the 101 rule, now with somewhere to descend *to*.

## 1.4 Pipeline: Proof-of-Work Marketing

In a credence-goods market flooded with claimants, claims are worthless and **demonstrations of judgment are the only marketing that converts.** You already manufacture them as engagement by-products; the pipeline job is to publish them:

- **The teardown** — public, anonymized versions of your kill decisions: "Client asked for X; here is the grid; here is why we refused and what we built instead, with the payback arithmetic." One honest teardown outsells fifty capability posts, because it proves the thing prospects can't verify: that you'll tell them no.
- **The eval report** — publishable scorecards ("what 96% accuracy actually means at 2,000 requests/month") that teach the buyer to demand evals — a demand only you, locally, can meet. You are commoditizing your competitors' vagueness.
- **Teaching** — workshops, office hours, the courses you're literally doing now. Teaching is positioning you can't fake and the audience pre-sorts itself into rung 1.
- **Referral engineering** — don't wait for word of mouth; schedule it. The retainer's quarterly review (101 L4) ends with a standing question to a satisfied client: "who else in [vertical] has this problem?" Asked at the moment the scorecard is green, it converts absurdly well, and the CAC is zero.

Instrument the funnel like any system: leads -> audits -> discoveries -> builds -> retainers, with conversion rates between stages. The number that matters is worked backwards: revenue target / average engagement value / conversion product = leads required per month. Most consultants have never done this multiplication, which is why their famine quarters surprise them. Yours won't (Lesson 3 makes this structural).

## Summary: The Rules

1. **A label anyone can claim is a queue, not a position.** If a competitor's name fits your sentence, the sentence says nothing.
2. **Position on one axis** — vertical, capability, or problem — chosen from your engagement data, not your aspirations. Narrow the marketing, never necessarily the revenue.
3. **Your 101 discipline is the storefront**: "acceptance defined by pre-agreed eval scores" is a differentiator competitors cannot copy by typing.
4. **Package the ladder**: audit -> discovery -> pilot -> build -> retainer. Each rung cheap to buy, standalone-valuable, and generative of the next. Descend scope, never rate.
5. **Publish judgment, not capability**: teardowns, eval reports, teaching. Proof-of-work converts; claims are spam.
6. **Work the funnel arithmetic backwards** from revenue target to required leads. Surprise famine is a math failure, not a market condition.

# Lesson 2 — Operations: The Kit, Leverage, and Quality Without Presence

## Why This Lesson Exists

Builder.ai spent a decade telling the market that software assembly was automated — an AI called "Natasha" would compose your app like ordering pizza. It raised over US$450 million, including from Microsoft, at a US$1.5B valuation. In May 2025 it collapsed into insolvency after a lender seized its accounts; reporting around the collapse alleged that revenue had been overstated by hundreds of millions via round-tripped invoices, and — the detail that made it a global story — that the "AI-powered" delivery had long leaned on large offshore engineering teams doing largely manual work behind the automated storefront. Whatever the final legal findings, the structural autopsy is plain: **the delivery model that was sold and the delivery model that existed were different machines**, and the gap was papered over with narrative until the cash ran out.

Solo consultants sneer at this story and then commit its miniature version constantly: selling "my personal judgment on your engagement" while quietly subcontracting delivery; selling "systematic methodology" while improvising every project from scratch; selling "we practice what we preach" while running their own back office by hand. Phase 2 scaling is precisely the art of changing your delivery machine *without* lying about it — and the two disciplines that make that possible are the kit and the eval, both of which you already own.

## 2.1 You Are the Bottleneck: The Audit

Before any hiring fantasy, run the measurement. Log two weeks of your working hours into four buckets:

```
  J  JUDGMENT     kill calls, architecture choices, red-lines, pricing,
                  the client-facing moments where trust is manufactured
  D  DELIVERY     building, prompting, eval harnesses, integration, docs
  S  SALES        content, calls, proposals, funnel
  A  ADMIN        invoices, scheduling, bookkeeping, inbox
```

The typical solo split is brutal: J is under 15%, though it is the only bucket clients are actually paying premium rates for and the only one that cannot be delegated on day one. D dominates — and D is exactly what the kit templates and what juniors, subcontractors, and (increasingly, as you know better than anyone) agents can absorb. A is pure deadweight that should be automated with your own tooling this month, for a reason beyond the hours: **your own back office is your cheapest, safest, most honest demo.** "Here is the intake agent that runs my firm" closes deals that case studies can't — it is the dogfooding proof Builder.ai faked. The strategic sequence is fixed: **automate A, templatize D, protect J, schedule S** — S dies silently when D is busy, and that silence is the feast-famine cycle's ignition (Lesson 3).

## 2.2 The Engagement Kit: Your Firm's Actual IP

Every 101 artifact you have produced is a prototype of a template. The kit is the versioned collection:

```
  ENGAGEMENT KIT v3.2                        (yes: version it like code)
  +-------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  /discovery    interview scripts, task-grid spreadsheet,    |
  |                doc skeleton w/ kill-criteria section        |
  |  /contracts    SOW clauses: eval-gated acceptance, change   |
  |                control, IP (L4), red-lines appendix         |
  |  /delivery     ref architectures per pattern (RAG, draft-   |
  |                dial WhatsApp agent, intake pipeline),       |
  |                eval-harness scaffold, guardrail checklist   |
  |  /operations   retainer scorecard, usage dashboard, post-   |
  |                engagement retro form -> feeds kit changes   |
  |  /sales        SKU one-pagers, teardown template, pricing   |
  |                calculator implementing the 101 ROI equation |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Three properties turn this from a folder of files into a firm:

1. **It compounds.** The post-engagement retro (mandatory, 30 minutes, no exceptions) asks one question: *what did we learn that changes a template?* Grid gains a column, a SOW clause gets tightened, a guardrail joins the checklist. Ten engagements in, your discovery is measurably better than any improviser's — that is margin and moat simultaneously. A firm that doesn't retro is a firm that pays full price for every lesson twice.
2. **It is the delegation interface.** You cannot hand a junior "do consulting." You can hand them "run the rung-1 audit: this script, this grid, this doc skeleton; I review before it ships." The kit converts your judgment from a performance into a curriculum.
3. **It is what an acquirer buys** (Lesson 4). A firm whose method lives in the founder's head is worth its receivables. A firm whose method is versioned, teachable, and demonstrably repeatable is worth a multiple.

And note the recursion you're uniquely equipped to exploit: the kit is a corpus, and you build retrieval and drafting systems for a living. The consultancy that RAGs its own kit — discovery drafts pre-filled from the last ten grids, SOWs assembled from the clause library — is running the exact machine it sells. That is the honest version of Builder.ai's pitch, and unlike theirs, it demos live.

## 2.3 Leverage: Hire, Subcontract, or Automate

Three ways to make delivery hours that aren't yours, ordered by commitment:

- **Automate** (first, always): your engineering-103 skills applied inward. Zero marginal cost, zero management load, and every internal automation is a sellable pattern. Ceiling: judgment-shaped work.
- **Subcontract** (second): variable cost, fast on/off, right for D-bucket overflow on well-specified builds. Rules: they work *inside your kit* on your accounts and templates, never client-facing without you, and the client knows a delivery team exists — the sold model must equal the actual model. You don't need to introduce every subcontractor; you do need your contract's staffing clause to permit them and your description of the firm to include them. The Builder.ai line is not "others help deliver"; it's "the storefront claims a machine the back room doesn't contain."
- **Hire** (last): fixed cost, training load, but compounding — an employee absorbs the kit and improves it; a subcontractor rents it. The arithmetic gate: hire when 3+ months of D-bucket overflow exceeds a salary, AND rung-1/2 SKUs exist for them to run semi-independently, AND your retainer base covers the new fixed cost even in a famine quarter (Lesson 3's "default alive" test). Hiring into spiky build revenue is how small firms die of one slow quarter.

What may never leave your hands in Phase 2: the kill decision, pricing, red-lines workshops, and the final review gate below. Delegating judgment before the kit can encode it isn't leverage; it's dilution.

## 2.4 Quality Control Without Presence

When you delivered everything, QC was implicit — you *were* the gate. At leverage > 0, quality must become explicit machinery, and you already know the shape of the answer because you sell it to clients: **eval-gate your own firm.**

- **Milestone gates**: nothing ships client-visible without passing its checklist — discovery docs have a rubric (grid complete? kill criteria objective? arithmetic checked?), builds must pass the eval harness *before* internal review, retainer scorecards are generated, not written. Your review time drops from re-doing to sampling.
- **The signature rule**: everything client-visible carries your standard, so the operating test for any delegated artifact is *"would I sign this?"* — and the kit's job is to make yes the default by construction rather than by heroics.
- **Instrument your own delivery** the way you instrument client systems: cycle time per rung, rework rate per subcontractor, eval-pass-first-time rate. When a number degrades, the retro asks whether the template or the person failed — usually the template.
- **The integrity invariant**, stated once, bluntly: at every moment, the description of your delivery machine that the client holds must be true. Scale changes the machine constantly; the discipline is updating the story in lockstep. Builder.ai is what the alternative looks like fully matured — the gap never stays small, because the gap is load-bearing.

## Summary: The Rules

1. **Measure yourself first**: J/D/S/A audit. Automate A this month, templatize D, protect J, schedule S before D fills the calendar.
2. **The kit is the firm**: versioned, retro-fed, compounding. A mandatory 30-minute retro per engagement is the cheapest R&D that exists.
3. **The kit is the delegation interface and the acquisition asset** — judgment converted from performance into curriculum.
4. **Leverage in order: automate, subcontract, hire.** Hire only past the arithmetic gate (sustained overflow + runnable SKUs + retainer-covered fixed costs).
5. **Dogfood visibly**: your own back office run on your own patterns is the demo that can't be faked.
6. **Eval-gate the firm**: milestone rubrics, the signature rule, delivery metrics, template-first blame in retros.
7. **The sold machine and the actual machine must be the same machine, always.** The gap is never temporary; it is load-bearing until it collapses.

# Lesson 3 — The Portfolio and the P&L: Services Arithmetic

## Why This Lesson Exists

In 1999, 37signals was a four-person web design consultancy in Chicago — good positioning (famous for a contrarian "less is more" stance), good clients, good rates. And structurally identical to every other agency on Earth: revenue arrived only when hours were sold, hours were finite, and every January the pipeline reset to zero. In 2004 they shipped Basecamp, a project-management tool built originally to manage their own client work — their internal kit, productized. Within a year Basecamp's subscription revenue passed the consultancy's; the firm exited services entirely and never returned. The founders spent the next two decades writing books about why.

Read as a systems story rather than a hero story, the interesting part is the *precondition structure*: the product came out of patterns seen across many client engagements, was funded by consulting cash flow, solved a problem the firm had verified dozens of times at full price, and was allowed to cannibalize the services business only after its recurring revenue could carry the payroll. Most consultancies that attempt this transition fail at exactly those preconditions — they build a product nobody verified, funded by hope, launched during a famine quarter. This lesson is the arithmetic layer underneath all of it: how a services P&L actually behaves, why it punishes success, and what the numbers must say before the 37signals move is anything but a gamble.

## 3.1 The Master Equation and the Utilization Trap

A services firm's revenue reduces to one line:

```
  revenue = capacity x utilization x rate x realization

  capacity     deliverable hours available (yours + leveraged)
  utilization  % of capacity billed
  rate         blended revenue per billed hour
  realization  % of billed value actually collected
```

Four levers, and beginners always pull the wrong one. Raising **utilization** feels safest — just work more — and is the trap. Recall Lesson 2's audit: S and kit-maintenance live in *non-billable* hours. Push utilization past roughly 70–80% and you are eating the hours that generate next quarter's pipeline and this year's kit compounding. The result is the feast-famine cycle, now visible as a mechanism rather than a mood:

```
  THE FEAST-FAMINE CYCLE (the default attractor of solo consulting)

     sell hard --> pipeline fills --> deliver hard (util ~100%)
         ^                                    |
         |                                    v
     panic-sell <-- revenue cliff <-- selling stops (no hours left)
                    (3-month lag: today's empty
                     pipeline is Q+1's empty calendar)
```

The lag is the cruel part: the famine is invoiced three months after the feast causes it, so the connection stays invisible unless you've done the arithmetic. The structural fix is a **utilization ceiling** treated as hard as an eval threshold — cap billable at ~70%, and schedule S and kit time as immovable blocks *first*. The growth lever you actually want is **rate** (via positioning, Lesson 1) and **realization** (via eval-gated acceptance, which is why 101's SOW machinery is secretly a realization tool: objective acceptance means fewer write-offs, disputes, and "just one more revision" hours).

## 3.2 Portfolio Construction: Default Alive

Your engagement mix is a portfolio with the same risk mathematics as any other. Three rules, each with a number attached:

1. **The retainer floor.** Define a services firm as *default alive* when recurring retainer revenue covers fixed costs (salaries, rent, tools, your own minimum draw). Below the floor, every famine quarter is existential and you will accept bad clients at bad prices out of fear — fear is the most expensive discounting mechanism there is. Above it, build revenue is upside and you can afford the kill decisions your reputation depends on. Every 101-L4 retainer you sign is not just revenue; it is negotiating posture for every future deal.
2. **Concentration ceiling.** No client above ~25–30% of revenue. Past that, they are not a client; they are an employer with worse benefits, and — they will eventually notice — a pricing veto. The fix is boringly mechanical: when one account grows, the funnel arithmetic from Lesson 1 must grow the denominator.
3. **The annual cull.** Once a year, rank clients by margin-adjusted revenue (invoiced value minus the true hour cost including the meetings, the scope skirmishes, the emotional overhead) and fire or re-price the bottom one. Consultants keep bad clients for the same reason engineers keep bad code — sunk familiarity — and the capacity freed by the worst client is always redeployed at a higher rate. The cull conversation is also, run kindly, the most honest rate-rise mechanism: "at renewal, the retainer is X+40%; here's the scorecard that justifies it; we understand if that changes your plans."

Rate rises deserve one more sentence, because solo consultants systematically under-raise: your kit compounds, your pattern library compounds, your eval evidence compounds — **your cost structure improves every quarter, and price should track the improved product, not your unchanged self-image.** The renewal, scorecard in hand, is the natural moment.

## 3.3 Cash: The Silent Killer

Profitable services firms die of cash timing constantly, and SME-market firms most of all. The kill pattern: fixed-price build, milestone-heavy at the end, client pays net-60, subcontractors paid net-15 — the firm is a bank making unsecured loans to its clients, involuntarily. Mechanics that prevent it, all boring, all non-negotiable:

- **Deposit on everything** (30–50% on builds; rung-1/2 SKUs 100% upfront — they're products, products are prepaid).
- **Milestones front-loaded and eval-gated**: the objective acceptance criterion from 101 exists precisely so a milestone payment can't be held hostage by vibes. Invoice on the eval report, not on "client satisfaction."
- **WIP as a monitored metric**: delivered-but-uninvoiced value is inventory rotting on a shelf. Weekly number, like any other system health metric.
- **The retainer smooths everything**: monthly recurring cash is the difference between surviving net-60 and dying of it.

Track one composite number monthly: **months of runway at zero new sales** (cash + contracted retainers, over fixed costs). Below 3, you're gambling; 6+ is where good kill decisions and hard price lines come from. Courage, in services, is a balance-sheet line item.

## 3.4 The Services-to-Product Transition: The 37signals Gate

Every consultant with a kit eventually stares at the same thought: *this pattern I've now built five times — that's a product.* Sometimes true. Usually a US$200k lesson in the difference between services and product economics:

```
                    SERVICES                    PRODUCT
  sale              relationship, weeks         self-serve/low-touch, CAC-driven
  revenue/customer  large (HK$100k+)            small (HK$500-5k/mo)
  customers needed  5-15                        hundreds+
  delivery cost     high, marginal              ~zero marginal, high fixed (R&D)
  feedback          in the room, free           instrumented, engineered
  failure mode      famine quarter              slow bleed you can't feel
```

The half-transition is the classic death: a "product" that still needs a discovery call, custom onboarding, and founder attention per customer has product prices with services costs — the worst cell of the matrix. The honest gate, distilled from the 37signals precondition structure, is four conditions, all required:

1. **Pattern verified >= 3–5 times at full services price** — clients paid real money for this exact shape repeatedly; the market research is invoiced, not surveyed.
2. **The kit already delivers it near-mechanically** — if delivery still requires your judgment per instance, you have a service with a logo, not a product.
3. **The retainer floor funds the R&D** — product development is paid for by default-alive recurring revenue, never by the famine-quarter fantasy that the product will arrive before the cash runs out.
4. **A named human owns product full-time past prototype** — a product built in the gaps between engagements is permanently a prototype; the gaps always lose to invoiced work.

And one strategic clarification that dissolves most of the agony: the transition is not binary. There is a stable, lucrative intermediate — **the productized-service firm with internal tooling** (your kit, automated, wearing a client-facing interface for delivery efficiency), which captures most of the margin gain with none of the CAC problem. Many firms that think they want to be SaaS actually want this. The full 37signals exit is only rational when condition 1's pattern is broad enough to sell *without* the relationship — which is exactly what makes it rare.

## Summary: The Rules

1. **Revenue = capacity x utilization x rate x realization.** Grow rate and realization; cap utilization at ~70% — S-time and kit-time are load-bearing, not slack.
2. **The famine is caused three months before it's invoiced.** Schedule selling as immovably as delivery.
3. **Default alive = retainers cover fixed costs.** Below the floor, fear does your pricing; above it, judgment does.
4. **No client over ~25–30%; cull or re-price the bottom client annually; raise rates at renewal with the scorecard in hand** — the product improves quarterly, so should its price.
5. **Cash rules are non-negotiable**: deposits always, milestones eval-gated and front-loaded, WIP watched weekly, runway >= 3 months minimum.
6. **The product gate is four conditions, all required**: pattern paid-for repeatedly, kit-mechanical delivery, retainer-funded R&D, dedicated owner. The half-transition is the worst cell in the matrix — and the productized-service intermediate is usually what you actually wanted.

# Lesson 4 — Trust at Scale, Failure, IP, and the Endgame

## Why This Lesson Exists

In April 2019, Hertz sued Accenture. The engagement — a complete redesign of hertz.com and its mobile apps — had consumed a reported US$32 million in fees before Hertz pulled it, alleging the deliverable missed both launch deadlines, wasn't the responsive design the spec required (Accenture allegedly quoted extra to make it work on tablets), had untested and defect-ridden code, and that Accenture then demanded millions more to fix its own work. Accenture disputed the claims; the suit settled quietly. Nobody remembers the settlement terms. Everybody in the industry remembers the complaint, because it was a public autopsy of every scaled-services pathology at once: acceptance criteria vague enough to litigate, scope interpreted opportunistically, delivery quality invisible until too late, and a trusted-advisor relationship converted into discovery documents.

Here is the Phase-2 reading: at 101 scale, trust lives in one relationship and you personally caught every problem before the client saw it. At firm scale — leverage, subcontractors, twelve concurrent engagements — **failures will reach clients**, and the difference between a Hertz-shaped ending and a deepened relationship is machinery you built *before* the failure: the acceptance mechanics, the post-mortem discipline, the IP clauses, and an honest answer to what your firm is ultimately *for*. This closing lesson is that machinery.

## 4.1 The Engagement That Fails Anyway

Run enough engagements and one fails — model behavior shifts, the champion quits mid-build, the data was worse than the audit caught, or you simply misjudged. The failure itself is survivable; the handling is what compounds or craters. The protocol:

1. **You announce it first.** The moment your instrumentation (retainer scorecard, usage metrics, milestone gates) shows the trajectory, you call the client — before they notice, before the invoice, before hope-based delay makes it a concealment. In a credence-goods market, detecting and disclosing your own failure is, paradoxically, the strongest competence signal you can emit; being *caught* is the weakest. Hertz's complaint reads as it does because problems surfaced at deadlines, not before them.
2. **The external post-mortem is a deliverable.** Blameless in tone, specific in mechanism, structured like your engineering post-mortems: what we expected, what happened, root cause, what the kit now does differently, what happens to this engagement. Handing a client a document that says "here is precisely how we failed you and what it taught our method" converts a percentage of failures into *deeper* retainers — the client learns what your firm does under bad conditions, which is the thing they could never verify at purchase time.
3. **The make-good is pre-decided policy, not negotiation.** Decide *now*, in peacetime: eval threshold missed at pilot end -> the 101 kill clause executes, client keeps dataset and report, final milestone is not invoiced. Production regression -> retainer hours absorb it x-fold. Your misjudgment at discovery -> re-run discovery free. Written policy converts a fee dispute into an execution of terms — the exact opposite of the "millions more to fix its own work" allegation.
4. **One failure updates the kit; it does not update the strategy.** Post-mortem discipline cuts both ways: overcorrecting into risk-terror after one blown engagement quietly kills the practice by making you decline everything interesting. The retro decides which template failed; the Master Rules stay put.

## 4.2 IP: Whose Kit Is It?

The kit compounds because every engagement improves it — which means every engagement's contract must permit that. Left unaddressed, standard work-for-hire language hands each client ownership of everything created for them, template improvements included; your compounding asset is being legally donated, engagement by engagement. The standard resolution, which belongs in your SOW template today:

```
  BACKGROUND IP (yours, forever)        FOREGROUND IP (theirs, forever)
  +------------------------------+     +--------------------------------+
  | the kit: templates, methods, |     | their data, their documents,   |
  | reference architectures,     |     | the eval dataset built from    |
  | eval harnesses, checklists,  |     | their examples, the deployed   |
  | prompt patterns, tooling     |     | configuration, their creds     |
  +------------------------------+     +--------------------------------+
                 |                                     |
                 +--------------- LICENSED ------------+
                   client gets a perpetual license to the
                   background IP embedded in their system;
                   you get the right to reuse learnings and
                   generalized patterns (never their data)
```

Two boundary disciplines. First, **learnings generalize; data never does** — the grid pattern from an F&B client informs the next F&B engagement; their menu, their customers, their eval examples do not travel, and saying this explicitly in the red-lines workshop builds more trust than silence. Second — your open-source instincts are relevant here — **the kit's *structure* can be public while its *depth* stays proprietary**: publishing the audit framework or the SOW clause pattern (rung-0 content, Lesson 1) costs you nothing, because the moat was never the template; it is the ten engagements of retro-fed refinement inside your copy. Open-sourcing the skeleton is marketing; the muscle doesn't photocopy.

## 4.3 Channels: Trust One-to-Many

Direct sales trades your hours for trust one relationship at a time. A **channel** borrows trust already accumulated by someone else: the accountant with 200 SME clients, the marketing agency whose customers keep asking about AI, the systems integrator with enterprise access but no eval competence. One relationship, many warm introductions — the only marketing more efficient than the retainer's quarterly referral question. Three structures, ascending intimacy:

- **Referral** (they introduce, you deliver as you, they take 10–15% of first-engagement value): cheapest to start, keeps your brand front-of-house, the correct default.
- **Subcontract-in** (you deliver under *their* brand): volume without CAC, but you are building their asset — their client, their retainer, their compounding. Acceptable as fill-in capacity; corrosive as a strategy, and note it makes *you* the back room in someone else's sold-vs-actual story — 2.4's invariant now depends on their honesty.
- **Co-branded practice** (formal partnership, shared SKUs — "the AI practice of [firm]"): the deepest and the stickiest; effectively a merger of reputations, so diligence them like an acquirer would — their delivery failure is now your Hertz complaint too.

The discipline all three share: **the channel's promises must stay inside your kit's proven patterns.** Partners, paid on introductions, will enthusiastically sell dial-4 moonshots you'd kill at discovery. The partner agreement therefore needs the same machinery as a client SOW — what may be promised, what must route through your audit, and your unencumbered right to run the kill decision on anything they bring. A channel that can override your kill decision has purchased your reputation on margin.

## 4.4 The Endgame: What Is the Firm For?

Three stable end-states, and the uncomfortable truth of Phase 2 is that Lessons 1–3 choices lock you into one long before you consciously choose:

```
                LIFESTYLE            AGENCY              PRODUCT CO
  optimize for  founder margin,      enterprise value,   recurring rev,
                freedom              sellability         scalability
  leverage      low (auto+subs)      high (team)         eng team
  kit's role    founder's tool       THE asset sold      becomes the product
  positioning   founder-brand OK     firm-brand only     category-brand
  ends in       great income,        acquisition or      the 37signals exit
                key-person discount  succession          or the slow bleed
```

What an acquirer of an agency actually buys — and therefore what "sellable" means concretely: **the retainer base** (recurring revenue with low churn), **the kit** (a method that demonstrably works without the founder), **the team** that runs it, and **the position** (the niche brand from Lesson 1). What they discount to near-zero: revenue that is really the founder's personal gravity. McKinsey's 2015 acquisition of QuantumBlack — a boutique data-science firm bought for its methodology, talent, and positioning rather than its headcount — is the pattern at the flattering end; the *key-person discount* is the pattern at the other, and a founder-branded, founder-delivered, founder-sold firm is, at exit, approximately a job with receivables.

None of the three states is wrong. The failure mode is *drift*: hiring like an agency while marketing like a lifestyle founder-brand while half-building a product in famine gaps — three endgames' costs, none's payoff. The Phase-2 closing discipline is one page, revisited annually with the same seriousness as the client cull: which endgame, therefore which leverage strategy, which brand carries the positioning (you or the firm), and what this year's kit investment is *for*. Every rule in this course serves any of the three; the page decides the weighting. Write it before Lesson 1 of 103 — because 103's frontier (agents eating the delivery layer, judgment as the residual product) changes the relative value of the three cells, and you'll want to know which one you're standing in when it does.

## Summary: The Rules

1. **You announce failures first.** Self-detection and disclosure is the strongest competence signal a credence-goods firm can emit; being caught is the weakest.
2. **The external post-mortem is a deliverable** and the make-good is pre-written policy. Fee disputes are what firms without kill clauses have instead of terms.
3. **One failure updates the kit, never the strategy.**
4. **Background IP is yours, foreground is theirs, learnings generalize, data never travels** — in the SOW template today, not negotiated per deal.
5. **Publish the skeleton, keep the muscle**: the kit's structure is marketing; its retro-fed depth is the moat.
6. **Channels borrow trust at scale, but a partner who can override your kill decision has bought your reputation on margin.** Partner agreements get SOW-grade machinery.
7. **Choose the endgame on paper, annually.** Lifestyle, agency, product — every Lesson 1–3 decision is a vote for one; drift pays all three costs for none of the payoffs.
